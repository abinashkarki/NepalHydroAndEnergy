# Storage Deficit

## Summary

Nepal’s energy crisis is often narrated as a shortage of projects. At the water–energy interface, the sharper truth is often a shortage of **seasonal inventory**: **storage deficit**—the gap between the impoundment capacity needed to re-time monsoon water to dry-season scarcity and what actually exists on the ground.

## Simple Explanation

Nepal gets most of its river water in the monsoon but needs electricity year-round. Storage means holding monsoon water in reservoirs to release it in dry months. Nepal has almost none compared to other countries, so even though it has lots of rivers, it cannot shift energy from wet to dry season.

## Run-of-river hydro and dry-season deficit

This is why adding more run-of-river hydro can worsen the dry-season deficit if the new MW arrives mainly in monsoon months and does not create storage or firm winter capacity.

## Orders of magnitude

International comparisons are sobering: Nepal is frequently placed around **~5 m³ per person** of storage, versus rough reference magnitudes such as **India ~180**, **China ~664**, **Norway ~15,100** (see [[data-storage-comparison]] for the project table and caveats). Nepal's operational hydropower storage is essentially **[[kulekhani-cascade]]** — three plants totaling **106 MW** ([[kulekhani-i|Kulekhani I]] 60 MW reservoir, [[kulekhani-ii|II]] 32 MW cascade, [[kulekhani-iii|III]] 14 MW tail-end) with original storage volume of **85.3 MCM** (JICA Storage Master Plan, 2014). Cumulative sediment deposition reached **25.3 MCM by 2010** (~30% loss). Post-2010 DGPS bathymetric surveys (Sangroula, NTNU/Hydro-Lab, 2009/2010) measured
**20.4 MCM total gross storage loss (23.9%)** and **14 MCM active live storage loss
(19.1%)**. Separate InVEST/RUSLE spatial catchment modeling (Bokan et al., 2021–2024)
tracked a declining watershed sediment delivery rate from 13.3 t/ha/yr (2002) to
4.8 t/ha/yr (2018) due to watershed management, but the cumulative loss is
irreversible. The "106 MW of storage" claim therefore overstates the system's actual
seasonal-shifting capability — effective live storage is likely **~59 MCM** and
still declining.

World Bank water diagnostics have framed multi-decadal storage needs at extraordinary volumes—on the order of **29.86 km³** in one national diagnostic framing—while noting that **effective seasonal storage for that purpose is effectively absent**. Whether one takes that headline literally or as an order-of-magnitude provocation, the ratio implies something like a **~200×** mismatch between aspirational storage need and operational reality: the exact factor matters less than the **sign**—Nepal is storage-poor in both water security and power-firming terms.

## Solar changes the storage job

[[solar-role-in-winter-deficit]] adds an important correction: not every dry-season kWh must come from reservoir water. Utility solar can supply a meaningful share of winter **daytime** energy faster and cheaper than new storage hydro. The storage deficit therefore splits into two problems:

- **Seasonal inventory** — water storage that shifts monsoon energy into dry months.
- **Daily shifting** — reservoir peaking, BESS, and demand response that move mid-day solar into the winter evening peak.

This makes storage no less important, but it makes a hydro-only storage story too narrow. Nepal still needs reservoirs where they are buildable; it also needs BESS and dispatch design so winter solar becomes firm enough to matter after sunset.

## Topography as paradox

Nepal’s steep gradients are good for **head** and therefore for RoR hydropower, but narrow **V-shaped valleys** work against **large gentle reservoirs** at acceptable inundation. The contrast with classic big-basin storage (e.g., Aswan-scale volumes from modest dam heights) is instructive: Nepal can get “big numbers” on paper from river energy, yet struggle to find **low-loss, low-conflict** reservoir geometries—see discussions around [[budhigandaki]] and [[dudhkoshi-storage]].

## Why storage is politically hard even when physically “obvious”

Seasonal mismatch ([[seasonal-mismatch]]) makes storage the clearest physical answer for evening and multi-week scarcity, but projects collide with **sedimentation**, **seismic hazard**, **resettlement**, **financing**, and **treaty-dependent** downstream politics. That is why [[buildability]] matters as much as MW: a reservoir is not a line on a map until the whole engineering–social–finance package survives scrutiny. [[tanahu-hydropower|Tanahu]] (140 MW, ~67% complete in the NEA FY 2024/25 report and roughly 75% complete in an April 2026 public update) is the test case for whether Nepal can deliver a reservoir at all — and [[kulekhani-i|Kulekhani's]] ~24% gross / ~19% live sedimentation loss is the warning for what happens after commissioning.

## Common Misunderstandings

- **"More dams automatically solve the problem"** — Nepal's narrow V-shaped valleys make large reservoirs hard to build. Storage is physically obvious but geologically and socially constrained.
- **"Storage means more total energy"** — storage re-times energy; it does not create new energy. The monsoon inflow is the same with or without a dam.
- **"Kulekhani proves Nepal can build storage"** — Kulekhani is small (60 MW + cascade) and has lost ~24% of gross storage to sediment. It is the exception that proves the scale of the gap, not a template for replication.

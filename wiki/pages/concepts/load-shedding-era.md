# Load Shedding Era

Nepal's electricity crisis of 2008–2018 is the historical backdrop against which every later development — the IPP boom, the trade transformation, the stranded generation problem, and the seasonal mismatch — must be read. The country did not drift into surplus from comfort. It broke out of a decade of forced power cuts that cost roughly a full year's GDP.

## The Crisis in Numbers

| Metric | Value | Source |
|--------|-------|--------|
| Peak daily load shedding | 14–18 hours/day (2009–2016) | World Bank CGE model ([[timilsina-cge-load-shedding-2021]]) |
| Total GDP lost 2008–2016 | ~US$11 billion | Timilsina et al. 2021 |
| Annual GDP loss (average) | ~7% of GDP per year at peak | Timilsina et al. 2021 |
| Annual cost (worst) | US$1.6 billion/year | World Bank ESRF |
| Investment suppression | Annual investment 48% below potential | CGE model result |
| Scheduled load shedding ended | 2018 | [[wb-country-economic-memo-2025]], [[nea-annual-report-fy2024-25]] |

The $11 billion cumulative GDP loss is roughly equivalent to Nepal's entire 2008 GDP. The era was not a footnote — it was a macroeconomic event comparable in scale to a major natural disaster, but spread across a decade.

## What Ended It

The end of scheduled load shedding was not a single policy decision. It was the confluence of three forces arriving together around 2018:

1. **Improved NEA dispatch and load management** — better scheduling, reduced technical losses, and more disciplined demand forecasting.
2. **IPP commissioning wave** — projects licensed during the 1990s and 2000s reached completion, adding hundreds of megawatts of new generation, most notably [[upper-tamakoshi]] (456 MW, commissioned 2021).
3. **Indian import access** — India's cross-border electricity trade guidelines (2016/2018) allowed Nepal to use Indian grid power as a dry-season backstop while domestic generation ramped.

## The End of Shedding Was Not the End of the Problem

Scheduled load shedding ended in 2018, but 76% of firms still reported regular power outages as late as 2022 ([[wb-country-economic-memo-2025]]). The shift was from *chronic shortage* to *fragile seasonal surplus* — a system that can export in the monsoon but still imports in the dry season, a system where generation curtailment replaces load shedding as the binding waste, and a system where the institutional architecture built for scarcity has not been reformed for surplus.

That transition — from shortage-era survival to surplus-era structure — is the arc the rest of this wiki traces.

## The Load-Shedding Legacy Today

The crisis shapes Nepal's electricity system in ways that persist:

- **IPP acceleration was a crisis response.** The state could not build fast enough, so private developers were brought in. The regulatory architecture (PPA terms, single-buyer model, AD penalties) was designed for shortage-era discipline, not surplus-era efficiency.
- **The industrial demand deficit traces to the shedding era.** Nepal's industry still burns coal and biomass because the grid was historically unreliable (14 hours/day of outages). See [[data-industrial-electricity-consumers]] and [[energy-substitution-pathway]].
- **The "reliability premium" persists in political memory.** Every policy conversation about export vs. domestic use, about storage vs. RoR, about solar vs. hydro, carries the implicit question: could this return us to 14-hour cuts?
- **NEA's institutional culture was forged in the shedding era.** The utility learned to control scarcity. It has not yet learned to manage surplus. See [[nea-triple-authority]].

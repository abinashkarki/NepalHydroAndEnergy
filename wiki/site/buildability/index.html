Summary

Buildability is the first-order filter on Nepal’s hydropower future: a project is not “good” because a desk study assigns a large MW number—it is good if the entire delivery package remains credible after geology, hydraulics, hazards, and grid realities are stressed.

Simple Explanation

Buildability asks: can this project actually be built and operated, given Nepal's mountains, earthquakes, sediment, and thin construction market? A project that looks good on paper may fail because the tunnel collapses, the turbines wear out in months from silt, or the transmission line to evacuate the power never gets built.

What buildability actually tests

A serious buildability review chains interdependent risks:

  • Geology and underground works — tunneling and cavern stability under high cover, fault zones, and variable rock mass ratings.
  • Sediment and abrasion — turbine technology, wear life, and maintenance cycles are not O&M trivia when concentrations swing orders of magnitude seasonally (see Sediment as Design Constraint).
  • Hydraulic transients and flood/GLOF exposure — hazard tolerance and intake resilience.
  • Evacuation — generation without Stranded Generation-grade transmission is incomplete infrastructure. See the Likhu cascade: four private IPPs totaling 213 MW commissioned into a New Khimti substation sized for half their output, stranding ~40% of contracted generation in FY 2023/24 until a 200 MVA transformer upgrade restored the corridor.
  • Execution realism — contractor capacity, local logistics winter, and financing covenants that survive delay. See Chameliya (11-year build, $7.5M/MW, headrace tunnel collapse) and Upper Trishuli 3A (2024 flood destroyed gates and control room, 88-day outage at Upper Tamakoshi).

Strategic implication

The planning-relevant metric is therefore system value — incremental dependable energy, loss factors, curtailment risk, and delivered cost — rather than headline megawatts. That reframing connects naturally to Storage Deficit (seasonal re-timing is worthless if the dam never reaches COD) and to basin-scale complexity in Karnali Basin megaproject corridors.

Megaprojects such as Budhigandaki Hydroelectric Project are instructive case files: the controversy is not only "how big," but whether the integrated package of reservoir, tunnels, communities, and finance can survive the decades-long path to operation. At the opposite scale, Marsyangdi (69 MW, 36 years old) demonstrates that aging units approaching overhaul age carry a different buildability risk. Khimti-I (60 MW, 1996) proves what an early-generation negotiated PPA can deliver, while Likhu-4 (120→52 MW redesign after India wheeling dispute) shows buildability includes the regulatory environment the project commissions into.

Common Misunderstandings

  • "Licensed = buildable" — many licensed projects stall for years due to financing, geology, or grid constraints. A license is a permit, not a construction guarantee.
  • "Buildability is just engineering" — it includes social acceptance, financing, regulatory environment, and market access.
  • "Big projects are inherently better" — megaprojects concentrate risk; a portfolio of smaller, buildable projects may deliver more system value.

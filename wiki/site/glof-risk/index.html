Summary

A glacial lake outburst flood (GLOF) is a sudden release of water from a glacier-related lake. The lake may sit on, beside or below a glacier and may be impounded by moraine, ice or bedrock. As the flood moves downstream it can entrain large volumes of sediment and debris, creating loads and channel changes that are poorly represented by ordinary river-flow records.

Simple Explanation

Glaciers and their surrounding landforms can create lakes with changing water levels, outlets and natural barriers. A sudden breach, overtopping event or internal drainage failure can release water rapidly. The moving flood may then erode its channel, collect boulders and sediment, form temporary blockages and produce additional surges far downstream.

Why It Matters in Nepal

Nepal's steep relief places settlements, roads, bridges, hydropower works and transmission assets along narrow river corridors below glacierised headwaters. Exposure is therefore not limited to infrastructure beside a lake. A project may instead be affected through flood-wave propagation, sediment bulking, channel avulsion, intake burial, scour or loss of access and monitoring systems.

For hydropower planning, GLOF exposure connects physical hazard to Buildability. Historical gauge records remain useful, but they do not by themselves describe every lake-breach or cascading-failure hydrograph. Project-level conclusions require site-specific lake, slope, sediment, routing and infrastructure-exposure studies.

Technical Explanation

Glacial lakes have different geometries and drainage controls. Some are dammed by unconsolidated moraine or ice; others occupy bedrock depressions or form on a glacier surface. Triggers and release processes can include avalanche-generated displacement waves, overtopping, erosion of a moraine, drainage through or beneath ice, and cascading release from one lake into another.

Downstream effects depend on more than the water initially stored in the lake. Steep gradients can accelerate the flow; erosion can add sediment and boulders; deposition can redirect the channel; and temporary ponding can create secondary surges. These processes link GLOF hazard to Sediment as Design Constraint and to the wider hydrological uncertainty described in Climate Change Increases Volatility and Resilience Risk, Not a Simple "More Water" Win.

Documented Recent Examples

2024 — Thame Valley / Dudh Koshi

The 16 August 2024 event was a documented two-lake cascade. A rock avalanche entered Upper Ngole Cho, generating a displacement wave and release that struck Lower Ngole Cho. The lower-lake breach was measured at about 21.6 m deep and 51 m wide; the resulting hyper-concentrated flow carried debris for more than 80 km and damaged the Khumbu hydropower intake and other infrastructure in Thame. These figures come from the later field and satellite investigation, not only the initial rapid assessment. See Thame Valley GLOF 2024 — Causes, Impacts and Future Risks.

2025 — Rasuwa / Lende–Bhote Koshi–Trishuli

On 8 July 2025, rapid discharge from a supraglacial lake on the Purepu Glacier sent a destructive flood through the transboundary Lende/Bhote Koshi and Trishuli corridor. NDRRMA's first situation report recorded damage to the border bridge, dry port, road and three hydropower plants while noting that detailed causal and damage investigations were continuing. See NDRRMA Rasuwa Glacial Flood Situation Report 1 (2025).

The 111 MW Rasuwagadhi plant had entered commercial operation in January 2025. NEA Engineering records severe civil, hydromechanical and electromechanical damage, suspension of generation, one unit returning on 6 December and two-unit generation under available dry-season discharge from 25 December. See NEA Engineering Company Annual Report FY 2081/82.

2026 — Langtang Lirung avalanche / Bhotekoshi–Trishuli

On 26 August 2026, an ice-rock avalanche from the northern slopes of Langtang Lirung into the Lhende Khola on the Tibetan side sent a debris-laden flood down the Bhote Koshi and Trishuli to the Narayani at the Indian border. The same corridor hit in 2025 was struck again: Rasuwagadhi — only partially restored from the 2025 damage — was reported completely washed away, Chilime was buried, and roughly 430 MW across 14–15 corridor projects was knocked out. A landslide-dam barrier lake then formed near the Chhochen Khola–Purepu Tsangpo confluence, creating breach risk over the same reach. See Reuters — Climate change boosted risk of devastating Himalayan flood, experts say (2026), Kathmandu Post — New barrier lake in Nepal raises fresh flood threat, China warns (2026) and the event page Bhotekoshi–Trishuli Flood 2026.

Note the causal-label discipline: this event is an ice-rock avalanche flood, not a documented glacial-lake outburst. The initiating mechanism must be established by investigation before applying the GLOF label — exactly the distinction the misunderstandings below describe.

Common Misunderstandings

  • "A GLOF is just a large monsoon flood." No. It originates from sudden release of a glacial lake and may develop a very different flood wave and sediment load.
  • "Every destructive flood involving glacier ice is a GLOF." No. Ice avalanches, rock-ice avalanches, landslide-dam failures and other high-mountain processes can generate debris-rich floods without a glacial lake outburst. The initiating mechanism must be established before applying the label.
  • "Only assets close to a glacier are exposed." No. Flood routing, erosion and sediment transport can carry impacts far downstream.
  • "A GLOF label proves that climate change caused the individual event." No. Regional glacier and glacial-lake change can alter hazard conditions, but attribution of a particular trigger requires event-specific evidence.

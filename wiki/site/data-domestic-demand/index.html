Simple Explanation

Nepal sells roughly 11,000 GWh of electricity per year. Over 90% of customers are households, but they use only 42% of the power. Industry — just 1.2% of customers — uses 37%. The grid is growing fast (+55% in 4 years) but per-person use is still tiny by global standards: 465 kWh per year vs 2,000–3,000 in middle-income countries. The biggest untapped demand is cooking: fewer than 1% of households cook with electricity.

Coverage / Method

  • Data sources: NEA Annual Report FY 2024/25, Distribution and Consumer Services Directorate (sectoral sales, consumer counts, provincial breakdowns); WECS Energy Sector Synopsis FY 2079/80 (per-capita consumption, agricultural energy cross-tab); World Bank Country Economic Memo 2025 and World Bank Economics of Household Cooking Using Electricity in Nepal (cooking electrification baseline).
  • Method: Sectoral shares from NEA DCSD sales tables. FY 2020/21 vs FY 2024/25 comparison uses same source methodology across both years. Per-capita consumption calculated from total sales and population estimates.
  • Known gaps: NEA does not report agricultural electricity separately (falls under "Others"); electric cooking figure (0.5%) is from the 2021 baseline and is corroborated only as "less than one percent" in the 2025 cooking-economics source, not annually updated; load profile is from monthly operational reports, not continuous metering.

Caveats

  • Single-fiscal-year snapshot: FY 2024/25 data; sectoral shares may shift as industrial load grows.
  • Cooking statistic remains a baseline: 0.5% electric cooking comes from the 2021 baseline. A 2025 World Bank-linked cooking-economics report still describes electric cooking as below one percent but does not provide a newer annual national share.
  • "Others" category is heterogeneous: Agricultural, street lighting, temporary, and water supply are combined; agriculture-only slice is estimated at 200–350 GWh.
  • Per-capita figures are approximate: Based on national population estimates, not metered consumer counts.

Finding

Industry is 1.23% of customers, 36.64% of consumption The revenue and sales shares are nearly identical (36.64% vs 36.52%), indicating industrial customers face average tariffs close to the system average (~NRs 9.44/kWh). Combined, commercial + industrial = 44.43% of sales from only 1.99% of customers. This concentration means changes among a small number of large industrial users can have an outsized effect on system sales. See Industrial Electricity Consumers in Nepal for the sub-sector breakdown.

Note

Data comparison notes FY 2020/21 figures are from NEA Distribution and Consumer Services Directorate as cited in earlier wiki pages (sectoral shares applied to ~7,277 GWh sales). FY 2024/25 uses the same source methodology (NEA DCSD). The two data points span approximately 4 Nepali fiscal years. Per-capita consumption rose from ~370 to 465 kWh over the same window.

Three pattern observations:

  1. Domestic and industrial grew at roughly the same pace (~10-11% CAGR). This is notable given the WB CEM finding that manufacturing output fell to ~6% of GDP — implying industrial electricity growth may reflect process switching (electric vs thermal) and/or new capacity rather than manufacturing expansion.

  2. "Others" is the fastest-growing category (+93%), driven by streetlight electrification, irrigation pump connections, and temporary construction supply — the long tail of grid extension.

  3. Domestic share is gently declining (43.1% → 42.0%) while industrial share is also declining (38.7% → 36.6%). The growth is in commercial, non-commercial, and others — the smaller categories gaining share as electrification deepens beyond basic household connections. This is structurally healthy: it means the grid is reaching firms, institutions, and public infrastructure, not just homes.

Caution

The import-to-exporter transformation is real but recent In FY 2020/21, Nepal imported nearly one-third of its electricity. By FY 2024/25, imports fell to 11% of available energy while exports hit 2,380 GWh — a structural shift driven by ~1,400+ MW of new IPP hydro commissioned in the intervening years. However, this net-export status is monsoon-weighted: dry-season imports persist at the margin. The transformation is fragile and depends on continued generation additions outpacing demand growth. System losses also improved from 17.18% (FY 2020/21) to 10.39% at the distribution level in FY 2024/25 — a meaningful gain.

Note

At 465 kWh/capita, Nepal is still roughly 5–7× below middle-income norms (2,000–3,000 kWh/yr) and ~60× below its theoretical hydropower potential per capita (~29,400 kWh/yr). The gap between current consumption and a plausible electrified economy is the central demand-creation opportunity in Domestic-Led Hydro Strategy.

Load Profile — Evening Peak Dominates

Monthly operational reports show peak demand consistently hitting 18:30–19:25 — the daily cooking-and-lighting window. This pattern persists in FY 2024/25:

  • System peak of 2,901 MW recorded on July 1, 2025 (13 Jestha 2082) — of which 2,214 MW was internal demand and 687 MW exported
  • National peak of 2,409 MW recorded on June 10, 2025
  • Winter peak hours still require import support: structural mismatch between RoR flat-ish output and evening spike

The structural features have not changed since FY 2020/21:

  • Run-of-river hydro generates at near-constant output (flow-following)
  • Demand spikes in the evening when people return home
  • The mismatch is most acute in dry-season evenings, when hydro output is at its annual floor and demand at its daily peak

This evening peak pattern is what makes storage + solar the physical complement to the hydro fleet. See Nepal Peak Load Curve FY 2024/25 for the annual load shape.

The Electrification Gaps — Where Demand Is Not Yet Created

Electric cooking: the largest untapped demand

  • Electric cooking used by 0.5% of households in the 2021 baseline; World Bank Economics of Household Cooking Using Electricity in Nepal still describes adoption as below one percent rather than replacing the baseline with a newer annual share.
  • Biomass (firewood) and imported LPG dominate cooking energy
  • NEA's FY 2024/25 report explicitly flags government policy to "encourage the use of electricity for cooking, transportation, and industry to reduce reliance on fossil fuels"

The current public corpus does not contain a metered national scenario for the electricity demand created by a 20–30% cooking transition. Energy Substitution Pathway discusses the substitution logic, but modelled scenarios must remain separate from observed demand.

Residential electricity still dominated by lighting

  • Household electricity use has tripled since 2010, but remains only ~5% of residential energy (the rest is biomass + LPG)
  • 92.2% of households used electricity for lighting in 2021 (up from 67.3% in 2011) — lighting drove the first wave of residential electrification
  • The second wave — cooking, heating, appliances — has barely started

Value-Capture Framing

Electricity captures higher value when it serves scarce periods (dry-season firm power, peak hours) and high-productivity uses. Nepal's current position:

  • Dry-season firm power: partially priced (storage PPA winter rate NPR 14.80/unit)
  • Export revenue: averaging NPR 7.11–8.72/unit in monsoon
  • Domestic industrial tariff: peak hours NPR 10.00–10.20/unit

Electric cooking is a large candidate demand pool, but its national load effect depends on adoption, appliance efficiency, meal timing, fuel stacking and feeder readiness. The copied public corpus does not contain a metered national conversion scenario. See Energy Substitution Pathway for substitution logic and Consumption Trajectory Outlook for forward growth; the argument for prioritising domestic electrification remains explicitly editorial in Twenty-Year Strategy and Domestic-Led Hydro Strategy.

Claim

India is the decisive external actor in Nepal's hydro future: primary market interface, frequent balancing partner, key transit state for regional trade (including Bangladesh pathways), treaty counterpart on shared basins, and a geopolitical filter on certain third-country developer linkages.

Evidence

Re-grounded 2026-11-04 in primary sources; the earlier research-ledger and source-review bullets were removed.

  • Main practical export market: In FY 2025/26 almost all of Nepal's 3,965 GWh of electricity exports went to Indian buyers, through the Indian Day-Ahead and Real-Time power markets, medium-term bilateral agreements (400 MW to Haryana, up to 200 MW to Bihar) and the Indo-Nepal Power Exchange Committee (NEA Annual Report FY 2025/26, PDF p.14). India is therefore the main practical export market: Indian market rules and prices set Nepal's realised export volume and revenue.
  • Dry-season balancing: The same year Nepal imported 1,171 GWh from India, 6% of energy available, concentrated in Poush–Chaitra (124–273 GWh a month) when domestic generation was lowest (PDF pp.13, 176). India is both the outlet for monsoon surplus and the supplier for dry-season shortfall.
  • Transfer limits are set bilaterally: NEA exported up to 1,200 MW and imported around 750 MW in FY 2025/26. The July 2026 India–Nepal Joint Working Group/Joint Steering Committee approved a combined Dhalkebar–Muzaffarpur/Sitamarhi limit of 1,650 MW export and 1,400 MW import, and interim 220 kV operation of Butwal–Gorakhpur with 200 MW export and 130 MW import (PDF p.14). Earlier wiki records gave 941 MW of project export approval and ~1,141 MW of approved cross-border transfer capacity (Nepal Energy Profile); both sit inside an India-mediated corridor regime.
  • Transit for third markets: NEA's FY 2025/26 report records "exporting a round-the-clock quantum of 40 MW power to Bangladesh starting June" (PDF p.55), which moves through the Indian grid, so India is the transit state for Bangladesh sales (Nepal–Bangladesh Electricity Trade Route).
  • Rules on who may sell: India's 2018 cross-border trade guidelines govern which entities may import or export (India Cross-Border Trade in Electricity Guidelines (2018)). Wiki sources report that in practice India blocks Chinese equity/EPC projects from selling into its market; the exact clause has not yet been located in a primary text in the corpus, so this point is supporting, not load-bearing.
  • Shared basins: Multi-gigawatt storage on shared rivers, such as Pancheshwar Multipurpose Project, depends on treaty-level agreement with India.

Confidence rationale

High. Restored on 2026-11-04 after re-grounding: the market, balancing and transfer-limit evidence comes from NEA's FY 2025/26 annual report. It is a structural claim about market size, geography, and institutional interdependence—not a forecast that every project requires Indian equity.

Unresolved issues

  • Bangladesh transit and trilateral commercial rules remain moving targets; "decisive" does not imply static tactics.
  • Domestic reform (Nepal Electricity Authority (NEA), open access) can expand non-Indian valorization paths somewhat, but cannot erase geography.

Boundary Conditions

  • This claim does not say Nepal has no agency. Nepal's agency operates through a structure in which India is dominant, not absent.
  • It does not say India controls every Nepali decision. It says India's market, corridor, and treaty role shapes the feasible set.
  • It does not say cooperation is impossible or that India is purely obstructive. It says the relationship is structurally asymmetrical.
  • It does not say every project requires Indian involvement. It says bankable export stories and shared-basin portfolios currently do.

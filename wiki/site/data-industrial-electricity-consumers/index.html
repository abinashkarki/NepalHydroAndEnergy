Coverage / Method

  • Data sources: NEA Annual Report FY 2024/25 (industrial electricity sales, consumer count, revenue, dues); WECS Energy Sector Synopsis 2024 (industrial total-energy cross-tab by fuel); World Bank Country Economic Memo 2025 (manufacturing analysis, captive diesel data).
  • Method: Industrial electricity from NEA DCSD sales tables. Industrial total-energy fuel mix from WECS sector × fuel cross-tab. Sub-sector electricity estimates constructed from (a) WECS total-energy cross-tab, (b) known process-energy ratios per industry, (c) rough plant counts and output volumes. No NEA sub-sector electricity breakdown exists in public documents.
  • Known gaps: NEA does not publish industrial electricity sales by sub-sector. Captive diesel consumption is estimated from WECS diesel-in-industry figure, not metered. The sub-sector GWh table is indicative, not verified.

Caveats

  • Sub-sector estimates are constructed, not primary: The Tier 1/2/3 electricity estimates are built from process-energy ratios and plant counts; actual NEA sub-sector sales data would significantly sharpen or revise these figures.
  • Single-year snapshot: FY 2024/25 for electricity; FY 2079/80 for total-energy fuel mix. Different years may not align perfectly.
  • Industrial dues are structural: NPR 29.42 billion outstanding (~74% of annual industrial revenue) suggests legacy arrears or ongoing payment difficulties that complicate demand-growth assumptions.
  • Captive diesel displacement is stylized: Assumes full reliability and grid availability; actual diesel displacement is constrained by outage frequency, grid extension, and industrial tariff structure.

Finding

Industry uses 11× more energy from non-electric sources than from the grid The 4,136 GWh of industrial electricity (~12,874 TJ) sits inside a total industrial energy consumption of 111,306 TJ. Coal + biomass + petroleum supply ~88.4% of industry's energy. The electrification of Nepal's industry — brick kilns, cement clinker, process heat — is still largely ahead of the country. See Energy Substitution Pathway for the displacement strategy.

Sum check

The above tiers sum to roughly 3,100–4,800 GWh, compared to the NEA-reported 4,136 GWh. The long tail of small industrial consumers — thousands of workshops, mills, and processors — fills the remaining gap.

Evidence gap

NEA sub-sector data NEA does not disaggregate electricity sales by industrial sub-sector in any public document examined. The table above is constructed from (a) the WECS total-energy cross-tab showing which fuels go to which industrial activities; (b) known process-energy ratios for each industry; (c) rough plant counts and output volumes. This is the single biggest data gap in the consumption profile. Obtaining actual sub-sector GWh data from NEA Load Dispatch Centre or Department of Industry would significantly sharpen the industrial-demand argument.

Structural Features of Nepal's Industrial Electricity Demand

1. Aggregate data do not show the consumer concentration

From the NEA consumer data: industrial consumers are 1.23% of all customers but consume 36.64% of all electricity. This implies an average of about 59,000 kWh per industrial connection per year, but an average cannot establish the distribution. The reviewed public sources do not identify the share taken by the largest consumers, the number of high-load accounts, or their sector mix.

Large industrial conversions may be high-leverage, but the claim needs an anonymised account-level load extract or a disclosed large-customer cohort before it can be quantified.

The next evidence threshold is an anonymised NEA or regulator extract with annual kWh, maximum demand, tariff class, location and industrial sub-sector for the largest accounts. It should be published in bands if customer confidentiality prevents plant-level disclosure.

2. Industry is underelectrified compared to peers

From the WB CEM: Nepal's industry consumed ~12,800 TJ of electricity in 2022 (IEA data), which was only 9% of final industry energy consumption. By comparison:

Country Industry Electricity Share Electricity Consumed (TJ)
Bangladesh 35% ~147,000
Nepal 9% ~12,800

Bangladesh's industry consumes ~11× more electricity despite a somewhat larger economy. The gap is not primarily size — it is electrification depth. Nepal's industry burns coal and biomass because the grid was historically unreliable (load shedding 2007-2017, 14 hours/day) and industrial tariffs didn't incentivize fuel-switching.

3. Captive diesel generation is a hidden consumption layer

The WECS cross-tab shows ~11,646 TJ of diesel consumed in the industrial sector — equivalent to 3,235 GWh of potential electricity demand if the grid were reliable. At NPR 130/L diesel and NPR 9.44/kWh average grid tariff, the per-kWh cost of captive diesel generation (NPR 25–35/kWh) is 3–4× the grid price. Yet firms pay it because outages still affect 76% of firms (WB CEM 2022 data).

This captive diesel load is the low-hanging fruit of industrial electrification. Each GWh displaced from diesel to grid electricity saves the firm ~NPR 15–25 million in fuel costs and saves Nepal the foreign exchange cost of importing that diesel.

4. Manufacturing has been shrinking, not growing

Manufacturing fell from 7.3% of GDP (conflict era) to 5.7% (repeated-shocks era). Construction overtook manufacturing as the largest industry subsector. This means:

  • Industrial electricity growth (~10% CAGR) is outpacing manufacturing GDP growth — suggesting the growth comes from electrification of existing processes (diesel → electric switching, electric grinding replacing coal-based processes) rather than from new factories.
  • If manufacturing recovers, industrial electricity demand could accelerate sharply — a positive-risk scenario for the demand-creation strategy.
  • If manufacturing continues to shrink, industrial electricity growth may slow — a negative-risk scenario.

Caution

Dues are a structural concern Outstanding industrial dues (NPR 29.42 billion) represent ~74% of annual industrial revenue (NPR 39.88 billion). This suggests either legacy arrears from the load-shedding era or ongoing payment difficulties in certain industrial segments. Either way, it complicates the "build demand to absorb surplus" thesis — the customers you need most may not pay reliably.

Industrial Consumption and Hydro

Hydro is 94.4% of installed capacity but only ~89% of available energy (imports supply 11% in FY 2024/25, mostly in dry season). For industrial consumers this means:

  • Monsoon period: abundant hydro, low marginal cost, reliable supply — the ideal time for industrial production
  • Dry season: hydro output drops, imports fill the gap at higher cost, and evening peaks strain the system — the reliability gap that caused 76% of firms to report outages in 2022

Industrial consumers that can shift production seasonally (e.g. food processing timed to harvest, construction materials for building season) benefit from the monsoon abundance. Industries that need year-round continuous power (cold storage, continuous-process cement, 24/7 steel mills) face the dry-season reliability gap and are the economic case for Storage Deficit and Solar–Hydro Complementarity build-out.

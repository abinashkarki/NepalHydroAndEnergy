Seasonal Mismatch

Why Nepal can experience wet-season electricity surplus and dry-season scarcity within the same hydro-dominated system.

Summary

Seasonal mismatch is the structural misalignment between Nepal's monsoon-peaking hydropower generation profile and its comparatively stable year-round electricity demand. It is the central problem of Nepal's RoR-dominated electricity system.

Simple Explanation

Nepal's rivers flood in monsoon (June–September) and shrink in winter (December–February). Its electricity demand stays roughly the same all year. So hydro plants produce too much in monsoon and too little in winter. This is not a drought or a shortage of dams — it is the physics of building a power system from rivers that follow a different calendar than human needs.

Common Misunderstandings

  • "Seasonal mismatch is a temporary drought problem." It is not. It is a structural property of RoR hydro in a monsoon climate, present every year.
  • "More dams will solve it." More run-of-river dams increase monsoon surplus without changing the dry-season floor. True seasonal storage is needed.
  • "Solar eliminates seasonal mismatch." Solar helps the dry-season daytime gap but does not cover the winter evening peak. It complements but does not replace storage.

Seasonal mismatch is the central structural problem of Nepal's hydropower-heavy system: inflow and demand follow different calendars. It is not a one-off drought story and not primarily a "we need more MW" story—it is what happens when a monsoon river regime meets a comparatively year-round electricity demand profile under a run-of-river–dominated fleet (see Run-of-River Hydropower).

The hydrograph vs the load duration curve

In Nepal's major rivers, a large share of annual discharge arrives in the wet season (commonly cited as 75%+ between June and September in basin planning discourse). RoR plants therefore produce a wet-season surplus and a dry-season deficit simultaneously—two faces of the same physics.

Operational anecdotes and system reporting cohere around a brutal swing: system-relevant generation often peaks in August–September in the ~2,200–2,500 MW range, then contracts in January–February toward roughly 600–900 MW—on the order of a 60–65% decline from the monsoon crest. Demand, by contrast, is comparatively smooth: winter heating and structural load growth do not "turn off" when the rivers fall.

The result is a recurring annual pattern: large monsoon surplus paired with material winter deficit. This is a structural property of RoR hydro in a monsoon climate, not a temporary market glitch.

Why "more RoR" amplifies both sides

Adding RoR capacity is not neutral: it increases energy when the system is already long, and it does little to change the dry-season floor unless accompanied by storage, diversified winter resources, or imports/exports that are actually available when domestic rivers are low. This is why debates about "potential MW" often miss the point captured in Nepal's Core Hydro Problem Is Timing, Not Annual Volume: timing can dominate volume in value.

Solar changes this page's implication. It does not abolish the mismatch, but it attacks the dry-season daytime component directly: Solar–Hydro Complementarity shows why Nepal's clear winter sky is a physical complement to its weak winter river flow. The remaining hard problem is the evening peak, which is why solar must be paired with Storage Deficit-class water storage, BESS, or demand shifting rather than sold as a stand-alone cure.

The seasonal generation pattern is visible across every NEA operating plant with multi-year data: Marsyangdi (69 MW, seasonal variation charts from FY 2081/82 show the monsoon-to-drought collapse even at 97% of design annual generation), Madhya Marsyangdi (70 MW, 107.6% of design), and Upper Tamakoshi (456 MW, 90.2% in a good year but 67% in a flood-shutdown year). The pattern is uniform — the amplitude varies, but the shape does not.

Fixes are physical, financial, or institutional—often all three

Only a small set of interventions change the mismatch itself rather than reshuffling who suffers it:

  • Storage that shifts water across seasons (Nepal's gap is stark: Storage Deficit)
  • Alternative winter sources (imports, solar plus storage, industrial demand response - each with constraints)
  • Market and dispatch design that prices scarcity and curtailment honestly—without pretending PRoR pondage is seasonal banking (see Seasonal Arbitrage Trap)

Basin-scale discharge framing and indicators belong in Basin Discharge Data. The analytical lens that separates "installed capacity" from what the system can depend on is Firm Power.

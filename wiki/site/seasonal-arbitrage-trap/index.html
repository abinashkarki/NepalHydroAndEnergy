Summary

The seasonal arbitrage trap describes how Nepal systematically sells cheap wet-season surplus electricity and buys expensive dry-season power, so headline trade balances mask destroyed margin on timing. The country can be a net exporter in energy terms while losing value on every unit traded.

Simple Explanation

Nepal sells electricity to India during monsoon when its rivers are full and prices are low. It buys electricity back during winter when its rivers are low and prices are high. Even if the total GWh exported roughly equals GWh imported, the money lost on expensive winter imports outweighs the money earned on cheap monsoon exports. The "trap" is that the timing of trade destroys value.

Common Misunderstandings

  • "Positive net exports mean Nepal is winning on trade." Not necessarily. Net energy volume and net revenue are different metrics; timing can make a net exporter a net payer.
  • "The IEX spread proves the trap is resolving." The IEX channel shows improvement, but whole-system averages still reflect negative spreads because non-exchange winter imports at peak-hour rates wash out exchange gains.
  • "Better negotiation with India would fix this." The trap is structural — it reflects Nepal's generation profile, not bargaining skill. Domestic storage or demand growth are the physical fixes.

The seasonal arbitrage trap describes how Nepal can appear to "win" on electricity trade while losing on economic value: the country systematically sells cheap wet-season surplus and buys expensive dry-season power, so headline trade balances mask a destroyed margin on timing.

The numbers that look like success

Reported averages illustrate the wedge: exports have averaged roughly NPR 7.11–8.72 per unit (depending on year and channel), while imports cluster around NPR 9.17–16 per unit—a large spread that reflects when each transaction occurs, not inefficiency in a single market. In FY 2023/24, Nepal exported about NPR 17.1 billion and imported about NPR 16.9 billion, leaving a net of only ~NPR 200 million despite being described as a "net exporter." In FY 2024/25, the headline improved — exports NPR 17.46B vs imports NPR 12.92B, net surplus ~NPR 4.5B. But the monthly pattern exposes the trap's persistence: first-half FY 2025/26 data shows peak monthly revenue of NPR 5.03B (Ashoj/Sep–Oct) collapsing to NPR 1.10B (Mangsir/Nov–Dec) before exports stop entirely and imports begin — a 4.6x swing within a single fiscal year (see Electricity Trade Time Series).

The monthly table: the trap made visible at full resolution

The NEA System Operation Department's FY 2079/80 (~2022/23) operational report provides month-by-month GWh data — the first full monthly resolution in the entire research corpus. Nepal's four biggest import months (Magh, Falgun, Chaitra, Baisakh) record zero exports. Baisakh alone logged 395.7 GWh imported, zero exported. A single peak winter month absorbs more import cost than any single monsoon month earns in export revenue.

A concrete dry-season snapshot: Falgun 2080 (mid-Feb to mid-Mar 2024) saw 406.3 GWh imported in one month, peak import hitting 643 MW on a single evening around 18:30–18:55 — the daily cooking-and-lighting peak — when run-of-river output was at its annual floor. Even with 3,591 MW installed, Nepal approved imports of up to 654 MW for Jan–Mar 2026 to cover winter peak demand.

See Electricity Trade Time Series for the full month-by-month breakdown.

Why this is structural, not accidental

The pattern is the trade manifestation of Seasonal Mismatch: a run-of-river–heavy fleet produces when rivers are high and must import when rivers are low. Without large-scale seasonal shifting — what Storage Deficit summarizes as Nepal's physical gap — arbitrage runs against the domestic system rather than for it. India's grid and approval corridors matter because they define when cross-border capacity is usable and at what effective price; see India — Energy Relationship with Nepal.

Two price realities: IEX vs whole-system

The trap operates on two layers that must not be conflated:

Exchange channel (IEX): Nepal's weighted-average IEX sell price exceeded its buy price in 2023-24 — ₹5.61/kWh sell vs ₹4.43/kWh buy, a +₹1.18/kWh positive spread. In 2022-23 the spread was negative (buy ₹5.95, sell ₹5.14 — a −₹0.81 penalty). The improvement is real: Nepal is competing more effectively in India's real-time market during high-hydro months.

Whole-system average: In the same FY 2023/24, Nepal's overall sale price averaged Rs 8.72/unit while purchase averaged Rs 9.17/unit — a negative spread of Rs 0.45/unit across the full portfolio. The gap between the positive IEX spread and the negative system average is explained by structural winter imports at constrained, high-cost rates (peak hours, non-exchange procurement) that wash out the monsoon exchange gains. Even in its best recent trade year, Nepal was a net payer per kWh when all transactions are averaged.

Policy signals: pricing dry-season scarcity

Power purchase and tariff design are beginning to encode scarcity by season. PPA restructuring for storage projects splits winter (~NPR 14.80/unit) from monsoon (~NPR 8.45/unit) — an admission that dry-season energy is a different commodity. Nepal's industrial time-of-day tariffs confirm the same logic: peak hours (17:00–23:00) at ~NPR 10.00–10.20/kWh vs off-peak at ~NPR 4.65–5.25/kWh. The system prices scarcity; it just cannot yet produce the peak supply from its own fleet.

Strategic implication

Nepal is currently resource-rich but value-poor because timing destroys the margin. The cleanest formulation: "The system is getting better at monetizing surplus, but it still pays a winter scarcity tax, and it has not yet built enough domestic electrification depth to turn hydropower into broad-based wealth."

A Domestic-Led Hydro Strategy reframes the objective: build and price for domestic dry-season reliability first, then use exports to monetize true surplus — rather than exporting because the alternative is curtailment at bad prices. Empirical trade dynamics belong in Electricity Trade Time Series.

Claim

Installed megawatts, annual generation, seasonal output shape, and firm (dependable) power are routinely conflated in Nepal's hydro discourse—producing misleading narratives about both "potential" and "solved" energy security.

Evidence

Re-grounded 2026-11-04 in primary sources; the earlier research-ledger and source-review bullets were removed.

  • "Potential" has several official definitions. WECS (2019) reports a revised gross potential of 72,544 MW and a techno-economic potential of 32,680 MW, while the legacy 83,500 MW figure is a theoretical estimate (WECS Assessment of Hydropower Potential of Nepal (2019), Hydropower Potential Categories). The three numbers answer different questions; none is a construction pipeline.
  • Installed capacity has several official totals. NEA reported 3,591.262 MW total and 3,389.912 MW hydro for FY 2024/25 (NEA Annual Report FY 2024/25) and 4,120.252 MW total for FY 2025/26 (NEA Annual Report FY 2025/26); the DoED operating registry stood at 3,791.874 MW in its April 10, 2026 snapshot and 3,909.721 MW in July 2026 (Hydropower Fleet Composition). These are different accounting scopes and dates.
  • Installed MW is not dry-season output. With 4,120 MW installed, Nepal's domestic generation in Falgun 2082 was 801 GWh, about 1,100 MW on average across the month, while national peak demand that year reached 2,479 MW (NEA Annual Report FY 2025/26, PDF pp.11, 176).
  • Peaking duration is measured in hours. NEA describes Kali Gandaki A (144 MW) as a six-hour daily peaking plant (Kali Gandaki A); daily pondage shifts energy within a day, not across seasons (PRoR Is Not Seasonal Storage).
  • Standard contracts price energy, not dependable capacity. The 2025 ERC/ECA discussion paper records energy-priced standard tariffs and discusses available capacity as a possible basis for a separate fixed payment (ERC/ECA Discussion Paper on Storage Hydro PPA Pricing (2025)). The regulator itself treats energy and dependable capacity as different products.

Confidence rationale

Medium-high. The distinctions themselves are high-confidence: each rests on WECS, NEA, DoED or ERC documents. How routinely public discourse conflates them is supported by the coexistence of several unlabelled "potential" and "capacity" figures, but the corpus does not yet hold a systematic sample of public statements. A documented sample of official speeches or plans that present one measure as another would justify high.

Unresolved issues

  • Need a pinned wiki normalization: which "potential" definition (theoretical, technical, economic, feasible) attaches to which public numbers, with citations per Hydropower Potential Categories and Hydropower Potential Pyramid.
  • Transmission and spill interact with "value of MW" in ways pure hydrology tables miss—see Stranded Generation.

Boundary Conditions

  • This claim does not say Nepal has "enough" installed capacity. It says installed capacity is routinely misread as if it were dispatchable or dependable.
  • It does not say all "potential" figures are wrong. It says the 83,000 MW rhetoric is often presented without clarifying which definition applies.
  • It does not say every project is a bad investment. It says the value of MW depends on when and how dependably it arrives.
  • It does not say Nepal has no energy shortage. It says the shortage is not primarily a volume problem.
